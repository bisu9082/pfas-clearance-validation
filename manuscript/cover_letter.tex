\begin{letter}{The Editors\\Journal of Hazardous Materials}
\opening{Dear Editors,}

We submit the research article \textit{``A testing-effort artefact in the
most-sensitive aquatic endpoint: structure-based PFAS screening recovers
predictive validity but still cannot support clearance''} for consideration in
\textit{Journal of Hazardous Materials}.

\textbf{Is the studied subject an environmental contaminant?} The work concerns
per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, analysed through 4{,}744 distinct measured
freshwater aquatic toxicity records for 87 parent structures drawn from the U.S.
EPA ECOTOX Knowledgebase, and applied to 17{,}619 untested structures from the
EPA PFASSTRUCTV6 inventory. The broader diagnostic uses 106{,}095 freshwater
acute records across 4{,}757 chemicals.

\textbf{Is the study conducted under environmentally relevant conditions?} We
answer this directly rather than by reference to the data being measured. PFAS
occur in surface water at ng/L to low \textmu g/L; the EU environmental quality
standard for PFOS is 0.65 ng/L and the US maximum contaminant level for PFOA and
PFOS is 4 ng/L. The effect concentrations modelled here, and the 0.1 to 1 mg/L
decision boundaries applied to them, sit several orders of magnitude above that
range --- as every hazard term does, since a predicted no-effect concentration
is an effect concentration divided by an assessment factor. The environmental
relevance of this work lies in what happens to that division: an assessment
factor is a multiplicative constant and therefore leaves a 4.9 $\log_{10}$ unit
effort gradient in the hazard term completely intact. A criterion derived at
nanograms per litre from a minimum-based hazard term inherits the whole of it.
We make this argument explicitly in \S3.7 and mark the occurrence range on
Figure~2c so that a reader can see where real concentrations sit relative to the
modelled ones. The required 100-word environmental implication statement
accompanies this submission as a separate document.

\textbf{A note on provenance.} This manuscript is a complete rebuild of work
previously declined by another journal, and we have treated the referees'
criticism of our data curation as correct. The per-substance table has been
regenerated so that structure standardisation precedes rather than follows
endpoint summarisation; exact duplicate records are now removed before testing
effort is counted; the structure-class analysis has been recomputed on the
present extraction, which removes a positive result we previously reported; and
a claim that representation choice does not matter has been withdrawn as
contradicted by our own deposited results. Each correction is stated in the
text where it applies rather than only in the corrected number. The central
finding is unchanged and slightly stronger after correction.

\textbf{The contribution.} Regulatory hazard screening conventionally summarises
a substance by the lowest effect concentration reported for it. We show that
this makes the response variable depend on how often the substance has been
tested: across PFAS the protective minimum correlates with record count at
$\rho=-0.75$, and subsampling to equal depth, compared against the correct
eligible-subset baseline, identifies 40--48\% of that as a pure
extreme-order-statistic artefact. Because a structure-based model cannot
predict testing history, this confound is by itself sufficient to produce the
external validation failures reported in the PFAS screening literature. Holding
a published model fixed and changing only how the same records are summarised
raises rank transfer on the same 58 structures by $\Delta\rho=+0.32$
(95\% CI $[+0.08,+0.57]$). Rebuilding training and
external sets consistently on an effort-stable endpoint restores external
validity and narrows the prediction interval --- and yet no untested structure
clears at a stringency-matched threshold.

We believe this is useful to the journal's readership in two ways. It identifies
a measurable defect in a widely used data-preparation convention, with a
diagnostic that any screening study can compute in one line. And it corrects the
interpretation of a negative result that is becoming established: structure-based
models are not failing on PFAS because they cannot represent fluorinated
chemistry, but substantially because they are being asked to predict a quantity
that encodes testing effort.

\textbf{Prior submission.} An earlier and materially different version of this
work, based on a curated subset of the aquatic record and without the
testing-effort analysis, was reviewed and declined by \textit{Environmental
Science \& Technology} (es-2026-093898). The present manuscript uses a different
data source, reports a different central finding, and reaches its conclusion by
a different mechanism; we mention the prior review in the interest of
transparency and can supply those reviews on request.

All extraction and analysis scripts, structure-locked tables and inventory-level
predictions are openly available and tagged as an immutable release. The
manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and is approved by
all authors. The authors declare no competing financial interest.
